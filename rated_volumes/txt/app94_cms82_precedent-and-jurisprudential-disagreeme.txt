Precedent and Jurisprudential Disagreement

This article examines how the legal principle of stare decisis (following precedent) works in constitutional cases, especially when Supreme Court justices hold different views on interpreting the Constitution. It argues that a flexible approach to stare decisis helps maintain stability in legal doctrine while still respecting the diverse legal philosophies of the judges.

# Precedent and Jurisprudential Disagreement

## Introduction to Stare Decisis

The article begins by noting how some observers have criticized the Supreme Court's tendency to overturn its own past rulings and have called for a stricter application of stare decisis ( the legal principle of following precedent ) in constitutional cases. However, this article suggests that the current flexible approach to stare decisis has a benefit: it supports doctrinal stability while still allowing for different legal philosophies among the justices. Stare decisis is supposed to help a justice decide whether to reverse a past decision or accept a previous error. Sometimes it does this, but often it helps manage strong disagreements between justices about the basic nature of the Constitution. Because justices do not all use the same interpretive methodology ( methods for understanding legal texts ), they don't always agree on what counts as an "error" in constitutional cases. When a controversial precedent is rejected, it doesn't always mean the case was wrong by its own standards; sometimes it means the justices simply disagreed with the underlying interpretive approach of that case. In these situations, "error" really stands for a fundamental jurisprudential disagreement ( a disagreement about legal theory or philosophy ). The argument in this article has three main parts. First, it explains the general outlines of stare decisis . Second, it argues that in controversial constitutional cases, a key, often overlooked, role of stare decisis is to mediate these jurisprudential disagreements . Recognizing this role provides a different way to understand what the flexible approach to stare decisis achieves in this type of legal precedent. On one hand, it avoids making specific solutions to methodological debates permanent. This shows respect for the diverse views both within and outside the Court, and it acknowledges that justices are unlikely to easily give up their deeply held interpretive beliefs. On the other hand, by making those justices who want to overturn a precedent responsible for justifying their decision, stare decisis helps control jurisprudential disagreement so it doesn't become too disruptive. A new majority cannot simply impose its view based on votes alone. They must defend their approach to the Constitution and be confident enough in that approach to warrant unsettling public reliance on existing law. If there is uncertainty about justifying a change, it encourages keeping the current legal standard.

## The Costs and Benefits of Weak Stare Decisis

Because the weak presumption of stare decisis allows for precedents to be overturned, it undeniably costs some legal continuity ( the state of being continuous over time ). However, this article points out that the importance placed on the strength of stare decisis is often exaggerated. Discussions about stare decisis usually treat horizontal stare decisis (the Court's duty to follow its own past decisions) as the only way to maintain doctrinal stability. But other parts of the legal system also contribute to this goal, often more effectively than horizontal stare decisis . For example, the prohibition on advisory opinions ( rulings on hypothetical cases ), the requirement for lower courts to follow Supreme Court decisions, the Court's certiorari standards ( rules for deciding which cases to hear ), its rule limiting the issues to those presented by the litigants, and the fact that the Court has multiple members with lifetime appointments all work together to provide continuity in the law. While overturning precedent can be disruptive, some instability in constitutional law is a natural result of pluralism ( a society with many different groups and ideas ). If there were more agreement about the nature of the Constitution—for example, whether it should be interpreted based on its original meaning ( originalist ) or as a living document that changes over time ( evolving )—we might expect greater, though still imperfect, stability. However, given the current reality, the level of stability we have experienced, or should expect, is limited, especially in highly divisive areas of constitutional law.

## Forms and Evolution of Stare Decisis

Stare decisis is a complex legal principle with many aspects. It started in common law courts and gradually became a fixture in federal courts during the 19th century. By the 20th century, it was well-established in the federal judicial system. However, its exact form and strength have not been fixed and can vary depending on the context. Stare decisis has two main forms: vertical stare decisis and horizontal stare decisis . Vertical stare decisis refers to a court's obligation to follow the decisions of a higher court. This is a strict rule with no exceptions. For example, a federal district court must follow decisions from its circuit court of appeals and the Supreme Court. Horizontal stare decisis , on the other hand, is a more flexible doctrine where a court follows its own past decisions. Its strength varies depending on the court. In district courts, it is almost nonexistent, as they typically do not feel bound by their own prior decisions. In courts of appeals, it is a nearly absolute rule, preventing one panel from overturning another, unless the entire court (en banc) agrees. In the Supreme Court, stare decisis is a softer rule, described as a matter of policy rather than an "unavoidable command." The strength of horizontal stare decisis also changes based on the type of precedent. The Supreme Court categorizes precedent into three types: Statutory precedents receive a "super-strong" stare decisis effect. This means decisions interpreting statutes are rarely overturned. Common law cases receive a medium-strength stare decisis effect. Constitutional cases are the easiest to overturn. The reason for giving constitutional precedent a weaker presumption of validity is that while Congress can correct mistaken interpretations of statutes by passing new laws, amending the Constitution to correct errors is a much more difficult process for the public.

## The Function of Stare Decisis Beyond Constraint

As this discussion shows, there is nothing predetermined about the form of stare decisis . It is a judge-made rule that federal courts have applied with varying force in different situations. This article particularly focuses on the force stare decisis should have when a Supreme Court justice faces a constitutional precedent with which they disagree. To be sure, stare decisis does more than simply restrict a judge's decision-making. Precedent influences decisions in every case by providing a framework for a justice to think about the problem they must resolve. Justices can more easily apply the broad language of the Constitution because precedent offers a structure for doing so. Justice Jackson's concurring opinion in Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer is a good example. Past cases allow justices to use analogy ( comparing similar situations ) to reason, and the doctrine itself provides arguments based on other legal methods like text, structure, ethics, prudence, and history. Because of these and many other contributions, stare decisis can fairly be called the primary tool of constitutional decision-making. However, the doctrine is most powerful when it prevents a justice from deciding a case in the way they might otherwise prefer. In such situations, a justice must decide, as Justice Brandeis once said, whether it is more important for the law to be "settled" or "settled right." This article focuses on that specific decision.

## Diverse Views on Overruling Precedent

Legal scholars have different opinions on how the Court should behave when deciding whether to overturn constitutional precedent. Those who support a weak form of stare decisis (allowing precedents to be overturned more easily) often do so because of their methodological commitments ( their preferred ways of interpreting the law ). For instance, some who believe the Constitution is a "living" document (known as living constitutionalists ) argue for the freedom to overturn precedents to allow for legal progress. Conversely, some originalists ( those who interpret the Constitution based on its original meaning ) argue for the freedom to overturn precedents to ensure the Constitution's original text takes precedence over established legal doctrine. However, those who favor a stronger, more robust stare decisis (making it harder to overturn precedents) do so because they value principles like judicial restraint ( judges limiting their own power ), the rule of law ( the principle that all people and institutions are subject to and accountable to law that is fairly applied ), and the legitimacy of judicial review ( the power of courts to decide if laws or government actions violate the Constitution ). This article develops an argument for weak stare decisis , but it is not based on the idea that any single methodological commitment requires this approach. Instead, it argues that the variety of such commitments on the Court makes a more relaxed form of constitutional stare decisis both unavoidable and probably desirable. This is especially true in cases where different interpretive methods clash. Before further developing this argument, it's important to clarify one point. While studies of stare decisis sometimes describe how the doctrine limits the Court as an institution, this article will look at the problem from the perspective of an individual justice. Each justice undoubtedly considers the institution's interests when deciding whether overturning a precedent is appropriate. However, before a decision is made, the Court as a whole does not have an institutional view on whether a precedent is right or wrong. Evaluating a precedent's consistency with the Constitution often depends on a justice's individual interpretive beliefs. For a justice who disagrees with a prior decision, the question is whether the limits of precedent outweigh those personal beliefs. Thus, while stare decisis serves institutional interests, this article treats its influence as primarily affecting individual justices rather than the entire Court entity.
