# Understanding Credit, Scores, and Reports

## Understanding What Credit Is and Why It Matters

Credit is a way to buy things now and pay for them later, like a loan from a bank that you promise to pay back. It's often used for big purchases, such as a home, car, or college tuition. Building good credit means making healthy financial choices and managing money well. Having good credit ( a strong history of paying debts on time ) can make it easier to get loans and better interest rates for important life events. When you get a loan today that you agree to pay back in the future, you are using credit ( the ability to borrow money or access goods/services with the understanding that you'll pay later ). It's a tool that allows you to buy things you might not be able to afford immediately, especially for important investments. Lenders, such as banks, prefer to give loans to people who are likely to pay them back. A positive credit history ( a record showing you consistently pay your debts on time ) is important because it helps you get loans and can also be used by other companies, like insurance providers or utility services, to assess your reliability.

## Your Credit Score and What Influences It

Your credit score ( a three-digit number that predicts how likely you are to pay back a loan on time ) is based on information from your credit reports. A higher score means less risk for lenders, making you a more attractive borrower. A lower score suggests more risk, which might lead to being denied a loan or getting a higher interest rate ( the cost of borrowing money ). The most common scoring system is the FICO® score , which typically ranges from 300 to 850. This table shows the general ranges for FICO® scores and what they mean: Room for Improvement : 300-550 Fair : 550-650 Good : 650-700 Excellent : 700-850 Several primary factors influence your credit score, though each affects it differently. These include: Payment history : How consistently you make payments on time. This is one of the most important factors. The amount of debt you owe : The total amount of money you currently owe to lenders. How long you've been using credit : The age of your oldest credit account and the average age of all your accounts. New or recent credit : How many new credit accounts you've opened recently. Types of credit used : The mix of different credit types you have, such as credit cards, installment loans, and mortgages. Your score can change as you open new loans or credit card accounts, use those accounts, and make payments.

## Your Credit Report: What It Is and How to Manage It

Besides a credit score, you also have a credit report ( a detailed statement about your credit activities ). This report shows your loan payment history and the status of your accounts. It contains personal information like your name, address, date of birth, and Social Security number. It also lists all your credit accounts, including current and past accounts, credit limits, balances, payment history, and creditor names. Public records, such as tax liens or bankruptcies, can stay on your report for 7 to 10 years. Independent companies like Experian, TransUnion, and Equifax create and keep these credit reports. It's a good idea to check your credit report regularly to correct any errors ( inaccuracies or mistakes ) as soon as possible. You are entitled to one free copy of your credit report every 12 months from each of the three main credit bureaus. You can request your free report online at annualcreditreport.com or by calling (877) 322-8228. You will need to confirm your identity with your name, birth date, address, and Social Security Number. If you find mistakes, first contact the company or lender that reported the information. If you can't resolve it with them, you can dispute the information with the credit bureau. Be sure to provide as much evidence as possible. However, if a negative item on your report is correct, there are no quick fixes. Be cautious of companies that promise to "fix" or "remove" correct negative items for a fee, as creditors are required to report accurate information.

## Strategies for Building and Improving Good Credit

Freddie Mac, a leading home loan company, recommends specific actions to build and keep a good credit history. These practices include: Making on-time payments ( paying your bills by the due date ). Keeping your total debt load manageable ( the total amount of money you owe is not too high ). Using a small portion of your available credit limit. Paying the full amount due, or at least more than the minimum payment. Reviewing your credit reports every year. Avoiding applying for too much new credit at once. To further improve your credit score, Freddie Mac offers additional tips: Pay your credit card bill in full if you can. Do not charge more than you can afford to pay back. Use only a small percentage of your credit limit (e.g., if your limit is 300). Keep only a few credit cards or credit accounts open. Pay down existing debts. Open and maintain credit accounts in your name to establish a history. Dispute any inaccuracies found in your credit report. Building or rebuilding credit takes time and patience. It's best to regularly review your credit and manage it wisely.
