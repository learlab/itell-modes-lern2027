Land Grant Institutions: An Overview

This brief provides an overview of land grant institutions in the United States, tracing their historical development, legislative support, and expansion over time. It highlights key acts like the Morrill Acts, Hatch Act, and the Equity in Educational Land-Grant Status Act, and discusses their impact on higher education access.

# Land Grant Institutions: History and Evolution

## Introduction to Land Grant Institutions

On July 12, 2022, we recognized 160 years since the 1862 Morrill Act ( a law that started federal support for higher education in the United States ) was passed. This act began federal support for higher education. Land grant colleges and universities ( institutions that received federal land or funds to support education ) show the country's past promise to make higher education available to more people. They do this by receiving federal money to create or expand state-funded colleges. These institutions, first funded by three different laws, offer affordable higher education focused on careers. This includes fields like agriculture, science, engineering, military studies, and the liberal arts. Today, there are 105 public and 7 private land grant institutions. They serve students in every U.S. state, Washington D.C., and five U.S. territories. This group includes 19 historically Black colleges and universities (HBCUs) ( colleges created to serve Black communities ) and 35 tribal colleges. This report, the first in a series about land grant colleges, looks at their history. It also examines the main laws that helped create and fund them. Over two dozen laws have approved federal money for these institutions. This report focuses on the main funding sources for the 1862, 1890 HBCU, and 1994 tribal land grant colleges and universities. Future reports will look at different funding sources, changes in money given, and staff salaries. They will also cover student numbers. Each report will specifically compare HBCU and non-HBCU land grant institutions.

## The Morrill Act of 1862 and the Hatch Act of 1887

The first land grant act ( a law providing federal support for colleges ), the 1862 Morrill Act, became law during the Civil War. President Abraham Lincoln signed it. This act gave each state 30,000 acres of public land for every senator and representative. States could sell this land to get money to create new public colleges or universities, or they could use the land to make existing institutions bigger. Congressman Justin Morrill first proposed this bill in 1857. However, President James Buchanan rejected it in 1859. The U.S. grew quickly in the early 1800s. To keep up, the new law aimed to increase research in farming and technical skills through colleges. This met national needs. The Morrill Act offered education to more people. It also marked a big step in the federal government getting involved in public education. The 1862 Morrill Act gave financial help indirectly. Later, the Hatch Act of 1887 ( a law that provided federal money for agricultural research ) gave direct federal money to land grant schools. This money was for creating and supporting agriculture experiment stations ( places for farming research ). These stations aimed to improve farming, ranching, and food production. Today, this funding is based on how many small farmers are in each state, so the amount changes yearly. The Hatch Act also requires states to provide a large part of the matching funds.

## 1890 Morrill Act and HBCU Land Grants

Mainly white men from middle and working classes benefited from the 1862 Morrill Act. After the Civil War, the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments changed the U.S. Constitution. They ended slavery, gave citizenship to all people born in the U.S., and expanded voting rights for Black American men. But even with these changes, Jim Crow laws ( state and local laws that enforced racial segregation ) in many states treated Black Americans as second-class citizens. After the Civil War, there were concerns about segregation in the former Confederate states. This led to the second Morrill Act of 1890 ( a law requiring states to provide land grant education for Black Americans ). This act made those states create land grant colleges for Black Americans. Or, they had to prove that their existing land grant colleges did not refuse admission based on race. States that did not offer land grant education to Black Americans, or refused to, lost federal funding. This 1890 act led to the creation of 19 HBCUs ( Historically Black Colleges and Universities ). Before this law, Black Americans had few choices for higher education. Older HBCUs, like Fisk University and Howard University, were usually private schools. Instead of land, the 1890 HBCU land grants received money directly. States that qualified received 1,000 each year after that, up to a maximum of $25,000 annually.

## Expanding Federal Support and Inclusion (1914-1977)

The Smith-Lever Act of 1914 ( a law that provided federal funding for agricultural extension services ) gave more federal money to the 1862 land grant schools. This act required them to share research from their agriculture experiment stations through a Cooperative Extension Service ( programs that share university research with communities ). Like the Hatch Act, the Smith-Lever Act also used a formula for funding and required states to provide matching money. The 1862 Morrill Act was updated in 1967 and 1972. This expanded land grant status to Washington D.C., American Samoa, Guam, Micronesia, the Northern Mariana Islands, and the Virgin Islands. These areas received federal money but no land. They also became eligible for research and extension funds from the Hatch and Smith-Lever Acts. The 1890 HBCU land grants do not get funds from the Hatch and Smith-Lever Acts. They first received federal money through the Bankhead-Jones Farm Tenant Act of 1935 ( a past law that helped tenant farmers and supported agricultural education ). Now, they get funds through the Evans-Allen Act of 1977 ( a law that provides funding for agricultural research at 1890 HBCU land grants ). This act gives them at least 15% of Hatch Act funds for farming research. Similarly, the National Agricultural Research, Extension, and Teaching Policy Act (NARETPA) of 1977 ( a law supporting agricultural programs at land grant institutions ) provides federal money to 1890 HBCU land grant colleges. These funds support agriculture extension programs. NARETPA sends this money directly to the colleges, not through the states, which is different from other laws.

## Tribal Land Grants and the Modern Land Grant System

The land grant system grew again with the Equity in Educational Land-Grant Status Act of 1994 ( a law that gave land grant status and funding to tribal colleges ). This act included 29 existing tribal colleges. It gave them a 1.7 million grant program for agriculture and natural resources. Each tribal college received 5 million for the Cooperative Extension Service. This was for states with both 1862 land grant colleges and 1994 tribal land grants to work together. Today, 35 tribal institutions have been designated as land grant colleges. Most of these tribal colleges are in the Southwest and Midwest. In 2019-2020, they enrolled about 23,000 students across 13 states. These colleges are special because they offer many services. These include high school equivalency (GED), developmental education, college degrees, and job training. They also serve as centers for community, economy, and child/elder care. Land grant colleges make up only about 3% of all public and private colleges in the U.S. But they have greatly helped increase access to higher education in every state. Most land grant institutions (52%) get funding from the 1862 Morrill Act. The next largest group (31%) are the 1994 tribal land grants, followed by the 1890 HBCU land grants (17%). Many states, like Oklahoma, have different types of land grant schools. These include 1862 land grants, 1890 HBCUs, and 1994 tribal land grant institutions. The land grant system has changed over the last 160 years. Many laws have helped it grow beyond its first goals. They have also added different ways to get money for teaching, research, and agriculture extension programs. In total, land grant institutions received $1.5 billion in federal money in 2019. This was for both ongoing ( capacity grants ( recurring federal funding based on a formula )) and project-specific ( competitive grants ( funding for specific projects that institutions apply for )) grants. Capacity grants are given regularly, based on a formula. Competitive grants are given for certain projects, and institutions must apply to get them.
