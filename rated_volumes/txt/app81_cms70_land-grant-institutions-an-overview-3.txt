Land Grant Institutions: An Overview

This brief outlines the history, legislation, and current status of land grant institutions in the United States, which began with federal support for postsecondary education through the 1862 Morrill Act.

# The Evolution and Impact of Land Grant Institutions

## Introduction to Land Grant Institutions

The 160th anniversary of the 1862 Morrill Act on July 12, 2022, marks a significant milestone in federal support for postsecondary education in the United States. This act initiated the country's commitment to democratizing higher education by offering federal support for establishing or extending state-sponsored postsecondary institutions. Funded through three legislative acts, land grant institutions provide students with affordable access to career-oriented higher education in fields such as agriculture, science and engineering, military science, and the liberal arts. Today, 105 public and 7 private land grant institutions operate across every U.S. state, the District of Columbia, and five inhabited U.S. territories. These include 19 historically Black colleges and universities (HBCUs) and 35 tribal colleges . This brief, the first in a series, explores the history of these institutions and the legislation that supports their funding, highlighting major sources for 1862, 1890 HBCU, and 1994 tribal land grants.

## Origins of the Land Grant System: The Morrill and Hatch Acts

The Morrill Act of 1862 , signed by President Abraham Lincoln during the Civil War, provided each state with 30,000 acres of public land per senator and representative. States could sell this land to fund a new public college or university or expand an existing institution. While U.S. Congressman Justin Morrill first introduced the bill in 1857, it was vetoed by President James Buchanan in 1859. The act's enactment reflected national interests in expanding research in agriculture and mechanical arts through postsecondary institutions, marking a major step in federal intervention into public education. Following the 1862 Act, the Hatch Act of 1887 provided federal appropriations to land grant institutions specifically for establishing and supporting agriculture experiment stations . These stations advance research in farming, ranching, and food production. Today, these funds are determined by a formula based on the number of small farmers in each state, leading to annual variations. ## Addressing Racial Disparities: 1890 HBCU Land Grants

While the 1862 Morrill Act primarily benefited white males, post-Civil War amendments extended citizenship and voting rights to Black American men. However, Jim Crow laws perpetuated systemic racial oppression, relegating Black Americans to second-class citizenship. Concerns over segregation in former Confederate states led to the passage of the second Morrill Act of 1890 , also known as the Agricultural College Act of 1890 . This act required states to either create separate land grant institutions for Black Americans or demonstrate that race was not an admission criterion for existing institutions; otherwise, federal funds would be withheld. This legislation led to the establishment of 19 HBCUs, providing much-needed postsecondary options as existing HBCUs were typically private. Unlike the 1862 land grants, the 1890 HBCU land grants received direct financial support, starting with 1,000 annually, capped at $25,000.

## Expanding the System: 1914-1977 Legislation

Further federal funding was allocated to 1862 land grants through the Smith-Lever Act of 1914 . This act mandated the dissemination of research from agriculture experiment stations via a Cooperative Extension Service at each land grant institution, also requiring state matching funds. The 1862 Morrill Act's reach expanded in 1967 and 1972 to include U.S. territories and jurisdictions like the District of Columbia, American Samoa, Guam, Micronesia, Northern Marianas, and the Virgin Islands. These entities received federal funds but not land, and qualified for research and extension funds under the Hatch and Smith-Lever Acts. While 1890 HBCU land grants were not eligible for Hatch and Smith-Lever Act funds, they initially received federal appropriations through the Bankhead-Jones Farm Tenant Act of 1935. Currently, they receive funds via the Evans-Allen Act of 1977 , which provides 1890 HBCU land grants with a minimum of 15 percent of Hatch Act funds for agriculture research. Additionally, the National Agricultural Research, Extension, and Teaching Policy Act (NARETPA) of 1977 provides federal funds for agriculture extension programs directly to 1890 HBCU institutions.

## Tribal Colleges and Modern Land Grant System

The land grant system further expanded to include 29 existing tribal colleges through the Equity in Educational Land-Grant Status Act of 1994 . This legislation authorized a 1.7 million agriculture and natural resources grant program, and 5 million to the Cooperative Extension Service to foster collaboration between 1862 land grant colleges and 1994 tribal land grants. Currently, there are 35 tribal institutions with this designation, primarily in the Southwest and Midwest. These tribal-controlled colleges enrolled approximately 23,000 students in 2019-2020 and are unique among land grants due to their wider range of services. These services include high school equivalency (GED), developmental education, college credentials, job training, and serving as community, economic, and child/elder care centers, reflecting a comprehensive commitment to their communities.

## Land Grants Today: Impact and Funding

Land grant colleges and universities, though comprising only about 3 percent of all U.S. postsecondary institutions, have significantly expanded access to higher education in every state. The majority (52 percent) are funded through the 1862 Morrill Act, followed by 1994 tribal land grants (31 percent), and 1890 HBCU land grants (17 percent). Many states, like Oklahoma, host multiple types of land grant institutions. Over the past 160 years, the land grant system has evolved through additional legislation, expanding its mission and diversifying funding streams to include teaching, research, and agriculture extension programs. In 2019, land grant institutions received a total of $1.5 billion in federal support through both capacity grants , which are recurring and formula-based, and competitive grants , awarded for specific projects through an application process. This ongoing financial support underscores their critical role in the U.S. educational and agricultural landscape.
