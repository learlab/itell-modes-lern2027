Review: Athens Through the Ages

This volume provides a critical review of Jennifer Tolbert Roberts's book, *Athens on Trial: The Antidemocratic Tradition in Western Thought*, exploring how Athenian democracy has been viewed throughout history and analyzing Roberts's arguments and interpretations.

# Athens Through the Ages Review

## Introduction to Athens on Trial

Jennifer Roberts's book, Athens on Trial: The Antidemocratic Tradition in Western Thought , explores the hostile tradition ( negative opinions ) about Athenian democracy. Roberts states that these negative views originated in ancient Athens itself. Despite occasionally using rhetorical gestures ( persuasive techniques ), Roberts's book does not claim to uncover a single, anti-democratic tradition. Instead, Athens on Trial offers a broad and well-researched chronological survey ( a historical overview in time order ) of Western opinions on Athenian democracy. This survey covers arguments both for and against Athenian democracy, from ancient times to the present. Roberts clearly favors democratic Athens and believes that modern societies can learn from its practices. A repeated idea in her book is that critics of Athens fail to recognize how democratic principles contributed to its creative achievements ( significant accomplishments ) (p. 312). However, Roberts, as an experienced historian, acknowledges the difficult questions about how much democratic Athens depended on imperialism ( expanding power through other territories ), chattel slavery, and the lower status of women. She does not always argue that critics of Athenian democracy are simply motivated by class-based opposition to popular rule.

## Ancient Athenian Critics of Democracy

Roberts's book is organized into three parts. The first part examines Athenian critics ( people from Athens who criticized the system ), particularly Thucydides, Plato, and Aristotle, who wrote about Athenian democracy. Roberts believes that the historical reputation of Athenian democracy suffered because it lacked a strong and skilled defender at the time. She sees this absence of native theorizing ( developing theories and ideas ) as a serious issue because the surviving passages praising democracy show that its supporters had very different ideas about the system's core principles compared to its critics (p. 35). To fill this gap, Roberts gathers texts from Herodotus, the tragedians and orators, Thucydides' Pericles, and Plato's Protagoras. She uses these texts to paint a picture of a vigorous regime ( a strong and energetic government ) that celebrated human involvement in politics and the arts. This portrayal shows Athenian democracy not just as a defense against tyranny, but as a dynamic entity driven by the combined effort and abilities of all its male citizens (p. 47). While finding strong ancient supporters of this "vital, throbbing entity" (p. 84) is difficult, there were many Greek enemies of Athenian democracy. These critics often shared a class-based critique ( criticism based on social and economic class ). They saw demo-kratia ( rule by the people ) as the tyrannical rule of the poor over the rich, believing that rich people made better political decisions (p. 49).

## Historical Perspectives on Athenian Democracy

The second part of Roberts's book looks at how democratic Athens was viewed by politicians, philosophers, and historians in ancient Rome ( the Roman Empire ), the city-states of Renaissance Italy ( independent cities in Italy during the Renaissance ), and Western Europe and North America during the 17th and 18th centuries. These periods involved debates surrounding the revolutions ( major political changes ) of those centuries. In separate chapters, Roberts explores what was said about Athenian democracy during each period. She also provides explanations for the different views expressed. Roberts emphasizes the wide variety of opinions, and her explanations are generally reasonable and fit the historical context. Unlike the first section, this part of the book does not suggest that critics of Athenian democracy are always class enemies of the people. The reviewer notes that Roberts faces challenges in accurately attributing anti-democratic views to figures like Thucydides, Plato, and Aristotle. She tries to maintain her argument against resistant texts by attributing carelessness ( lack of attention ) or inconsistency ( not staying the same ) to these authors. For example, she suggests Thucydides did not think deeply about constitutional questions, and she downplays Plato's critiques of oligarchy, framing him as a typical Greek aristocrat. She also explains Aristotle's praise for collective deliberation as an inconsistency in his generally aristocratic tradition, rather than acknowledging a nuanced position (p. 90).

## Modern Scholarship on Athenian Democracy

The final third of the book focuses on more professional and scholarly works ( academic writings ) from the 19th and 20th centuries. Roberts provides an excellent overview of this period. She describes the eventual triumph ( victory ) of those who supported Athenian democracy over its critics. Roberts identifies two main reasons for this victory. First, the German Romantics ( a cultural movement in Germany ) like Herder, Schiller, and Hölderlin sought to find a unified way of life, or a "country of the mind," as a remedy ( solution ) to the fragmentation caused by modernity. Second, George Grote , a utilitarian scholar and friend of the Mills, significantly contributed to this shift. Grote's History of Greece established democratic Athens as the true origin for the secular liberal democracy ( a government system based on individual rights and freedoms, separate from religion ) he hoped to see in England. Roberts uses these historical uses of the Greek past as starting points. She then discusses 20th-century writings on Athenian democracy in detail, emphasizing the complex interaction between scholarship ( academic study ) and political vision ( ideas about how society should be governed ).

## Critique of Roberts's Philosophical Interpretations

The reviewer, Stephen G. Salkever, points out that Roberts is not the most skilled reader of philosophical texts ( writings on fundamental questions of existence and knowledge ) among current ancient historians. Her comments on discussions of Athens by Machiavelli, Rousseau, and Hegel are similarly weak. Salkever argues that Roberts extracts only clear pro- or anti-Athenian democracy sentiments from their works, ignoring the broader philosophical goals and questions of these writers. For example, she describes Machiavelli as "a man of powerful passions" whose emotions were "intensely engaged by the political turmoil," leading him to have "mixed emotions in Machiavelli" (p. 124). For Hegel, she suggests he "cannot seem to make up his mind" about the figures of Antigone or Creon (p. 218). This philosophical naïveté ( lack of experience or wisdom in philosophy ) means Roberts treats philosophers as either one-sided political supporters or narrow academics. Salkever believes this approach not only misrepresents philosophy but also removes the deeper philosophical content from politics and scholarship. Despite these shortcomings in interpreting philosophical treatises, Athens on Trial provides valuable material for distinguishing which critical questions about Athens and democracy are still relevant today. The book's weaknesses as an interpretive treatise ( a formal written work explaining a subject ) do not lessen its significant value as a compendium ( a collection of information ) that will interest anyone studying modern uses of ancient Greek politics and philosophy.
