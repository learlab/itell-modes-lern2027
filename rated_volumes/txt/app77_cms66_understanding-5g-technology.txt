# Understanding 5G Technology

## Introduction to 5G Networks

The 5G network is the fifth-generation technology standard for broadband cellular networks , globally deployed by cellular phone companies starting in 2019. It serves as the planned successor to 4G networks, with predictions indicating over 1.7 billion subscribers worldwide by 2025. Like earlier generations, 5G networks are cellular networks where service areas are divided into small geographical cells. All 5G wireless devices within a cell connect to the internet and telephone network via radio waves through a local antenna. ## Advantages of 5G Technology

The primary advantage of 5G networks is their greater bandwidth , leading to significantly higher download speeds, potentially reaching up to 10 gigabits per second (Gbit/s). This increased bandwidth allows 5G networks to serve more than just cell phones; they can function as general internet service providers for laptops and desktops, competing with existing ISPs, and enable new applications in the Internet of Things (IoT) and machine-to-machine communication. However, 5G networks require 5G-enabled wireless devices for access. One key benefit is its use of small-cell technology , which allows for denser data transmission in smaller spaces compared to 4G. Additionally, 5G utilizes high-frequency signals that travel faster than lower-frequency signals found in older networks. This contributes to minimal latency , meaning almost zero delays in acquiring information, greatly enhancing real-time applications like gaming.

## Challenges and Disadvantages of 5G Deployment

Implementing 5G technology faces several challenges. First, it requires the use of different frequencies and carriers, which can increase radiation levels and necessitate greater EMF protection . Second, high-frequency signals travel only short distances, meaning 5G networks require many more antennas and multiple access points for stable connections, particularly in buildings. Third, the advanced innovations of 5G technology are expected to make it more expensive than 4G, raising concerns about its overall cost. Fourth, limited availability is a significant issue; 5G is not yet available internationally or in many parts of the world, partly due to carriers' reluctance to undertake the extensive infrastructure changes required. Finally, device compatibility is a major hurdle; most current devices are compatible with 4G or 3G, meaning new 5G-enabled models are necessary, and even these may experience increased battery drain. Furthermore, health issues have been raised, with studies suggesting that 5G may increase radiofrequency radiation levels near antennas. The 2011 IARC review, for instance, confirmed that RF radiation is carcinogenic to humans, implying potential harmful effects from 5G.
