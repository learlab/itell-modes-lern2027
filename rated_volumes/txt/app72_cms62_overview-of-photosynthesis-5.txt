## Introduction to Photosynthesis

Solar-powered mechanisms are ubiquitous, from calculators to living organisms. While humans don't directly use sunlight, plants capture its energy, converting it into chemical energy stored in sugars like ATP. Organisms capable of producing their own chemical energy are called producers , such as plants, bacteria, and protists, forming the base of Earth's food webs. Photosynthesis is the process that captures energy from sunlight to make these energy-storing sugars, essential for nearly all life. Plants absorb visible light from the sun's radiant energy for this conversion.

## Photosynthetic Organisms and Chlorophyll

Producers form the base of Earth's food webs, generating chemical energy for themselves and other organisms. Animals obtain energy either directly from plants or indirectly through consuming other organisms that feed on plants. This vital energy origin traces back to sugars made through photosynthesis. Chlorophyll , a molecule within chloroplasts (shown in Figure 4.4), absorbs specific red and blue wavelengths of visible light, reflecting green light. Plants contain chlorophyll a and b . While green is reflected, other molecules capture some green light, making plants appear green. ! Chloroplasts in plant cells contain a light-absorbing molecule called chlorophyll.

## Photosynthesis Occurs in Chloroplasts

Chloroplasts are the membrane-bound organelles in plant cells where photosynthesis takes place, particularly in leaf cells. Photosynthesis proceeds in two main stages within the chloroplast. The chloroplast's two essential parts for photosynthesis are the grana and the stroma . Grana (singular, granum ) are stacks of coin-shaped, membrane-enclosed compartments called thylakoids . The membranes of the thylakoids contain chlorophyll, other light-absorbing molecules, and proteins. The stroma is the fluid that surrounds the grana inside a chloroplast.

## Stage 1: Light-Dependent Reactions

The light-dependent reactions constitute the initial phase of photosynthesis, directly capturing energy from sunlight. These crucial reactions unfold within and across the intricate membranes of the thylakoids found inside the chloroplasts. Water (H₂O) and sunlight serve as the essential inputs for this stage. Within the thylakoid membrane, chlorophyll absorbs sunlight's energy, which is subsequently transferred. During this process, water molecules are broken down, leading to the release of oxygen (O₂) as a valuable byproduct. The captured light energy is efficiently converted into vital energy-carrying molecules, such as ATP (adenosine triphosphate), which are then transferred to power the subsequent stage. ! ## Stage 2: Light-Independent Reactions and Overall Process

The light-independent reactions , also known as the Calvin cycle , form the second stage, using energy-carrying molecules (like ATP) from the light-dependent reactions to build sugars. These reactions occur in the stroma of chloroplasts. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) molecules are incorporated into a chemical cycle. Using the transferred energy, these reactions construct larger molecules, forming a simple sugar, typically glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), to store captured sunlight energy. The overall equation for photosynthesis is: `6CO₂ + 6H₂O --(light, enzymes)--> C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂`. This shows carbon dioxide and water as reactants, yielding a six-carbon sugar and oxygen. Plants use these sugars for growth and to build starches and cellulose. Oxygen is vital for other organisms, making photosynthesis indispensable for both plant life and the broader ecosystem. !
