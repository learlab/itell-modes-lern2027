## Roberts's Survey of Anti-democratic Thought

Jennifer Roberts's Athens on Trial focuses on the "hostile tradition about Athenian democracy" with roots in ancient Athens itself. The book is an uneven yet useful and extensively documented chronological survey of Western opinions on Athenian democracy, encompassing both pro- and anti-democratic perspectives from antiquity to the present. Roberts explicitly sympathizes with democratic Athens and those who believe contemporary society can learn from its model. A recurring theme is that critics often overlook the connection between democratic ethos and significant creative achievement. However, Roberts, as an experienced historian, also acknowledges the complex dependencies of democratic Athens on imperialism , chattel slavery , and the subordination of women , avoiding the argument that critics are solely driven by class-based opposition.

## Critiquing Ancient Philosophers

Roberts divides her book into three sections, with the first analyzing Athenian critics, specifically Thucydides , Plato , and Aristotle . She posits that Athenian democracy suffered historically because it lacked an ardent theoretical advocate, unlike its critics. Roberts attempts to fill this gap by presenting passages from Herodotus , tragedians, orators, and Plato 's Protagoras to depict Athens as a dynamic entity, energized by its male citizens. However, the reviewer challenges Roberts's interpretations, finding her on "shaky ground" when attributing an antidemocratic mold to these philosophers. Roberts accounts for counter-evidence by suggesting these authors were careless or inconsistent, as seen in her claims that Thucydides lacked deep thought on constitutional questions, Plato's attacks on oligarchy were insignificant due to his aristocratic background, and Aristotle's praised collective deliberative capacity was a result of inconsistency.

## Athenian Democracy in Roman to Early Modern Thought

The second section of Roberts's book surveys diverse accounts of democratic Athens from ancient Rome , Renaissance Italy, and Western Europe during the 17th and 18th-century revolutions. In separate chapters, she examines opinions about Athenian democracy and offers contextual explanations for these views. Her approach emphasizes the extraordinary range of perspectives presented. Unlike the first section, this part of the book does not assert that critics of Athenian democracy are inevitably class enemies of the people . The reviewer finds Roberts's explanations in this section sensible and well-contextualized, commending her survey of a wide array of historical views on Athenian democracy.

## Nineteenth and Twentieth-Century Interpretations

The final section of the book delves into 19th and 20th-century scholarly works , providing an excellent overview. Roberts identifies two primary sources for the eventual triumph of pro-Athenian democracy views over its critics. First, the efforts of German Romantics (e.g., Herder, Schiller) sought a coherent "country of the mind" as an antidote to modernity's fragmentation. Second, the project exemplified by the utilitarian scholar George Grote , a friend of the Mills, established democratic Athens as the true ancestor of liberal democracy. Roberts's narrative discusses the complex interplay between scholarship and political vision in 20th-century writing on Athenian democracy.

## Assessing Roberts's Philosophical Nuance and Overall Contribution

The reviewer notes that Roberts is not the most sophisticated reader of philosophical texts. Her comments on discussions of Athens in the works of Machiavelli , Rousseau , and Hegel are deemed inadequate, as she extracts only straightforward pro- or anti-Athenian democracy sentiments, ignoring their broader philosophical aims. This approach caricatures philosophy and diminishes the inherent philosophic content of politics and scholarship. For instance, she attributes Machiavelli's "mixed emotions" to passions and questions Hegel's inability to "make up his mind." Despite these interpretive shortcomings, Athens on Trial successfully provides considerable material for distinguishing valid critical questions about Athens and democracy from those that are not. The book's compendium merit makes it valuable for anyone interested in modern appropriations of ancient Greek politics and philosophy.
