Post-Traumatic Stress, Resilience, and Post-Traumatic Growth

This document explores three key concepts related to trauma: Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), Resilience, and Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG). It defines each concept, explains their interrelationships and differences, and outlines approaches to prevention and intervention.

# Understanding Post-Traumatic Stress, Resilience, and Post-Traumatic Growth

## Introduction to Trauma Concepts and Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

When studying trauma, three main ideas help us prevent, respond to, and support Service members and their families affected by war-related trauma. This document explains each idea, how they are connected, and how they are different. It also describes ways to prevent and treat Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) ( a mental health condition triggered by a terrifying event ). PTSD is like a psychological injury from very upsetting experiences. A 2016 study predicted that about 10% of veterans might experience PTSD over the next decade. The study also estimated that it could take 40 years to deal with the mental health effects of war. Recovery from PTSD depends on how severe the trauma was and a person's past history with trauma. Most people have strong reactions to trauma but usually get better without treatment. However, if someone is diagnosed with PTSD and doesn't get help, their symptoms can get worse over time. Trauma that can lead to PTSD includes war or combat, natural disasters, car crashes, terrorist attacks, the sudden death of a loved one, rape, kidnapping, assault, sexual or physical abuse, and childhood neglect.

## Military Trauma and PTSD Symptoms

The medical view of PTSD focuses on life-threat trauma ( a single, very scary event ) that causes a natural fight, flight, or freeze response. This type of trauma can often be treated with specific therapies. In military settings, PTSD can also develop from cumulative 'wear-and-tear' ( long-term stress from repeated difficult situations ) or from moral injury ( when someone is involved in or witnesses acts that go against their deep moral beliefs ). These complex situations may need different treatments than currently available. PTSD has four main groups of symptoms: Re-experiencing : Reliving the event through nightmares or flashbacks. Avoidance and Numbing : Staying away from thoughts, feelings, or places connected to the trauma. Hyperarousal and Reactivity : Being easily startled, having sleep problems, or feeling constantly on edge. Negative Thoughts and Mood Changes : Having bad thoughts about oneself, feeling guilt, or having a generally negative outlook. A normal response to trauma becomes PTSD when you are stuck. After a traumatic experience, your mind and body are in shock. With PTSD, you remain in psychological shock. Your memories and feelings about what happened get disconnected. To move on, you need to face your memories and emotions. Individuals who show early symptoms after trauma, even if not full PTSD, may still have significant problems. Not addressing these early symptoms increases the risk of developing full PTSD.

## Understanding Resilience

Resilience ( the ability to recover quickly from difficulties ) is often seen as a personal trait that helps someone go through a traumatic event without major lasting changes. A resilient person usually stays psychologically and physically healthy during and after a trauma. It can also be described as a steady path of healthy functioning after a difficult event. People who handle traumatic events well without developing severe PTSD symptoms are considered resilient; they show toughness in tough times. Bensimon (2012) offered three ways to understand resilience: Recovery : Returning to a pre-trauma state, often called 'bouncing back' or 'bouncing forward.' This might involve minor PTSD symptoms that don't become severe. Resistance : Avoiding harmful behavior changes caused by trauma, maintaining mental balance. This doesn't mean PTSD is fully preventable or that those with PTSD are flawed, but some personalities are better equipped to resist trauma. Reconfiguration : Using mental effort to change and grow from the experience, which is similar to Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG). Resilience is complex and not a simple, unchanging trait. A person might be resilient at work but show stress at home. It comes from many different factors, not just a few. Resilience can also apply to families, organizations, communities, and societies.

## Exploring Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG)

Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG) ( positive psychological change experienced as a result of trauma ) is a concept developed by R. G. Tedeschi (2011) and his team. PTG happens when someone goes through a traumatic event and grows beyond their previous state. A key part of PTG involves challenging one's core beliefs about the world after trauma, which can lead to both stress and personal growth. The more a person challenges their core beliefs, the more likely they are to experience PTG. It's the mental effort of dealing with trauma, not the trauma itself, that causes PTG. Another important idea is rumination ( thinking deeply or repeatedly about something ). There are two types: intrusive rumination ( distressing, unwanted thoughts ) and deliberate rumination ( purposeful thinking aimed at understanding and solving problems ). Intrusive rumination is linked to a higher chance of PTSD, while deliberate rumination is strongly linked to PTG. A 2017 study found that while both types of rumination can be present in PTSD and PTG, deliberate rumination had a stronger connection to PTG among veterans. It's important to know that PTG and PTSD can occur at the same time, and growth can happen even when someone is very distressed.

## Domains of PTG and Approaches for Treatment

PTG shows itself in five key areas: New Opportunities : Feeling that the traumatic experience opened up new possibilities. Changed Relationships : Developing closer bonds with some people or a deeper connection with others who have suffered. Increased Personal Strength : Believing more in one's own abilities, thinking, 'If I survived this, I can face anything.' Greater Appreciation for Life : Having a deeper appreciation for life itself. Spiritual Change : A significant change in one's belief system or increased spirituality. PTG doesn't mean trauma is good, but it acknowledges that trauma is often an unavoidable part of life, especially in military service. Not everyone who experiences trauma or PTSD will achieve PTG, and it's not realistic to expect all Service members with PTSD to experience this kind of growth. Military organizations, researchers, and medical professionals are working to prevent and treat PTSD, aiming to also increase resilience and PTG. Their research focuses on two main areas: psychological programs ( therapies and training that focus on mental and emotional health ) that help prevent and treat PTSD, and pharmacological solutions ( treatments involving medication ) for managing the condition.

## Psychological Interventions for PTSD

Research shows several psychosocial factors ( social and psychological influences ) linked to resilience in adults. These include using skills to manage emotions, facing fears, problem-solving, being physically active, using humor, and having strong social support. Most of these factors can be taught to Service members and veterans to help prevent and treat PTSD, and also promote PTG. The U.S. Military has developed programs, like the Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness program, which uses principles of Positive Psychology ( a field focusing on strengths and well-being ) to build resilience. This program helps Service members use their strengths, develop positive emotions, build supportive relationships, and find meaning even in difficult times. For veterans with PTSD, similar programs can improve these factors. Many efforts to build resilience and PTG have focused on rethinking traumatic events, increasing social support, physical activity, positive emotions, and finding purpose. One such program is Goal-Directed Resilience in Training (GRIT) , which teaches life skills for goal-directed actions that align with a person's values. GRIT helps people manage PTSD symptoms while working towards their goals, improving their belief in themselves, promoting positive emotions, and boosting cognitive skills. Studies have shown GRIT can improve PTSD symptoms and overall well-being. Other newer programs for preventing and treating PTSD include: Life skills education : Teaches problem-solving, decision-making, and emotional control. Meaning-making activities : Help people find purpose in life by integrating their trauma into their beliefs. Mindfulness practice : Teaches staying in the present moment and noticing feelings and thoughts. Yoga : Combines physical activity with mindfulness, often paired with meditation. These strategies can help promote PTG and can be used with PTSD treatment. However, individuals need to have certain cognitive abilities for PTG. Those with traumatic brain injury or substance use problems might need these issues addressed first before benefiting from PTG interventions.

## Pharmacological Solutions and Overall Summary

Beyond psychological programs, pharmacological solutions ( treatments involving medication ) are also being studied for PTSD. Horn et al. (2016) reviewed research on how PTSD affects hormones, genes, and the immune system. This area of research is new but shows promise as a treatment option, either alone or combined with psychological therapies. To summarize, PTSD will likely remain a significant challenge for many active duty Service members and veterans. Both resilience and PTG play important roles in preventing and recovering from PTSD. Effective psychological and medication-based treatments are available. A complete approach to intervention and treatment is best, addressing both PTSD and PTG. This approach involves understanding trauma, improving emotional control, sharing one's story, linking that story to the five domains of PTG, and developing life principles to handle future difficulties.
