# An Introduction to Carbon Capture and Storage

## Introduction to Carbon Capture and Storage

Carbon capture and sequestration/storage (CCS) captures carbon dioxide (CO₂) from power generation and industrial processes, storing it to prevent atmospheric release. CCS significantly reduces CO₂ emissions, with some facilities capturing 90-100%. This explainer covers CCS technology, its operation, U.S. usage, deployment barriers, and policies.Captured CO₂ can also be used in manufacturing or industrial processes, a practice called Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS) . Emission reductions vary based on permanent storage or displacement of other CO₂ sources. A primary use is enhanced oil recovery (EOR) , where injected CO₂ helps extract oil and is simultaneously sequestered underground. Selling CO₂ for EOR can provide revenue, incentivizing CCS.

## The State and Mechanisms of Carbon Capture and Storage

The Global CCS Institute's 2019 Status Report indicates 40 million metric tons of CO₂ are captured and stored globally each year from operational or under-construction plants. In 2018, the United States emitted over 5 billion metric tons of CO₂. Globally, 51 large-scale CCS facilities are operational or under construction, with 10 in the U.S. "Large-scale facilities" are defined as capturing 800,000 metric tons/year for power plants or 400,000 metric tons/year for industrial sites.! Map of large-scale operational CCS facilities in the United States CCS deployment involves three steps: capture , transportation , and storage . Capture technologies include post-combustion (power plants), pre-combustion (industrial processes, often new facilities), and oxy-fuel combustion (burns fuel in pure oxygen for concentrated CO₂). After capture, compressed CO₂ is transported to a storage site via pipelines, ships, or vehicles. Finally, CO₂ is inject into deep geological formations for long-term storage, such as old oil/gas reservoirs, saline formations, or coal beds.

## Cost of CCS Implementation

A major barrier to CCS deployment is the high cost of technologies , primarily for capture and compression. Capturing CO₂ can reduce plant efficiency and increase water use, making projects financially nonviable. (Water use is also problematic in water-scarce regions.)CCS is an early-stage technology, making financial returns risky. Investors demand higher risk premiums , increasing capital costs. Mitigating this risk is vital to incentivize CCS investment. Research and development are needed to optimize technology, which the Department of Energy's Carbon Capture Program is addressing.

## Challenges in CO₂ Transportation and Storage

CO₂ transportation presents significant challenges and costs. Compressing CO₂ and maintaining pipeline pressure requires substantial energy, and pipelines themselves are expensive. Specially designed pipelines are essential due to CO₂'s pressurized nature and the risk of impurities causing leaks or explosions. Connecting sources to suitable storage sites via pipeline can be difficult and costly, especially in areas lacking appropriate geological formations. Geologic storage availability isn't a major short-to-medium-term barrier, with ample capacity globally and in the U.S. A 2018 IPCC report affirmed processes for "very long-term storage." However, underground CO₂ injection poses a potential for seismic activity . Researchers are exploring minimization strategies, including above-ground carbon dioxide mineralization .

## Public Support and Policy Framework for Carbon Capture and Storage

Public support is crucial for widespread CCS implementation, but perceptions are not fully understood. Influencing factors include acceptance of fossil fuels' role, pipeline construction, perceived safety of CO₂ transport/storage, and CCS effectiveness. Further research is needed to gauge public reaction to major CCS deployment.Policies that increase demand and reduce costs are essential to accelerate CCS, as highlighted by the IPCC's Special Report on Carbon Dioxide Capture and Storage . Carbon pricing, public investment, subsidies, and clean energy standards are key.In the U.S., national tax credits under Section 45Q of the Internal Revenue Code incentivize carbon sequestration. State-level tax credits exist in California, Texas, Louisiana, Montana, and North Dakota. Proposed legislation, such as the Launching Energy Advancement and Development through Innovations for Natural Gas Act, could further boost CCS.
