Changing Issues in Leisure-Family Research

This paper examines the historical evolution of leisure-family research, from initial neglect to periods of consensus and critical analysis, highlighting shifts in focus, methodologies, and understanding of family structures. It also proposes future directions for the field.

# Evolution of Leisure-Family Research

## Introduction to Leisure-Family Research

John R. Kelly, a researcher at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, first presented an analysis of the evolving topics in leisure-family research in 1982. This work aimed to shift research focus toward understanding the interplay between individual choices and social structures, personal expression and social roles, and the balance between freedom and obligation in leisure. Kelly also stressed the need to study the increasing diversity of family forms and life transitions, moving beyond traditional family life cycle stages. He highlighted the importance of exploring intimacy, various expressions of sexuality, and the 'identity ambivalence' experienced by young mothers and others navigating social change. Even after thirteen years, many of these initial challenges remain, and new issues have emerged. To better understand this field, Kelly proposes examining leisure-family research through three distinct historical periods, each characterized by different research goals and methods of analysis.

## From Neglect to Consensus in Leisure Studies

In the Period of Neglect during the 1960s and 1970s, researchers paid little attention to the family in leisure studies. Most studies used surveys of occupational groups and focused on large groups of people, often linking leisure to work. However, by the end of this period, some research began to return to studying households, putting leisure into a broader social context. The Affirming Period of Consensus followed in the early 1980s. During this time, the individual became the main focus of analysis. Researchers started to accept that most leisure activities happen at home and with other family members. It became clear that leisure involved social aspects, such as expressing and developing relationships. The connection between leisure and family became widely accepted, almost taken for granted. This view was largely positive, suggesting that leisure and family benefit each other. Studies explored how leisure created 'bonding,' 'affection and intimacy,' and helped with developmental play . Leisure was seen as contributing to family 'solidarity.' While a few researchers noted some tensions, especially for women balancing enjoyable activities with family duties, the main focus remained on the traditional nuclear family, believing that families playing together was beneficial. Leisure was primarily understood as a source of individual satisfaction within the family context, without much differentiation based on gender or social class. However, some early signs of disagreement started to appear.

## The Critical Period: Diversity and Repression in Family Leisure

The Critical Period in leisure-family research, spanning the late 1980s and 1990s, brought about significant changes. Researchers began to seriously consider gender as a factor. The focus also shifted more and more to the individual within specific social contexts. A new understanding developed: family leisure was not just about promoting freedom and bonding; it also involved 'surplus repression.' This means that societal pressures and expectations could limit or control individuals' leisure choices within the family. To understand this period better, three main issues were explored. First, researchers started to give fuller attention to the variations and challenges families experienced, even starting in the early 1980s. Topics like divorce, the combined impacts of poverty and ethnicity, and diverse sexual orientations made it clear that focusing only on the traditional nuclear family was not enough. Researchers had to acknowledge the varied and unpredictable paths people's lives take. Second, there was a growing awareness of the 'lived conditions' of life within real and diverse social contexts. Life often involves disruptions, traumas, and the ongoing effort to rebuild. Leisure and family were recognized as deeply connected to social contexts, not just individual experiences. This meant research could no longer assume that most families were straight, white, and middle-income.

## Differentiating Factors and Emerging Family Definitions

In the Critical Period, researchers began to identify differentiating factors that shaped leisure experiences. The most important factor was gender , leading to a closer examination of women's leisure. Adult women's leisure could not be simply categorized by activity or meaning; instead, it was understood as a complex interplay of meaning and restrictions, often influenced by societal beliefs that were patriarchal (male-dominated) and repressive. A key change was the increasing involvement of most women in the paid workforce, which led to role conflicts . This 'stalled revolution' was more than just a lack of time; it highlighted deeper cultural and political values. Simply identifying women's 'constraints' evolved into finding signs of resistance even within repressive situations. The old functional model, which assumed leisure always benefited the family, was broken. Stimulated by the focus on gender, other areas of awareness developed. One was the reality of gay and lesbian family forms , which required a new definition of 'family' itself. Researchers also recognized heterosexual couples in committed relationships without marriage or children . This opened up discussions about all forms of sexual expression, not just those tied to traditional marriage and family. Additionally, the growth of single-adult households , with or without children, became a significant social fact. These developments spurred new ways of looking at social processes and structures. New theories, including feminist , queer , and ethnic perspectives, challenged older academic assumptions. The field also became more aware of the importance of social class , multiple cultures , and age . Critical approaches now connect these differences to broader social changes impacting the workforce, family composition, and even how leisure is bought and sold. For economists, the family is seen more as a unit of consumption than production.

## Guiding Principles for Future Leisure-Family Research

The family continues to be a major topic in public discussion, but leisure is often overlooked in these debates. Despite this, researchers should be careful not to get caught up in political discussions that might limit open dialogue. Here are some general hints for future leisure-family research. First, researchers should avoid focusing on single issues . No single factor, like sexuality, gender, class, or ethnicity, can explain everything important. Life also has both positive and negative aspects. Using dialectical models —which consider opposing ideas—can better explain things, for example, balancing community with alienation, bonding with violence, or love with exploitation. Simple models and assumptions should be avoided, as real life involves both conflict and agreement. Second, researchers should examine their own 'domain assumptions,' or implicit ideologies , that guide their studies. It's not always easy to recognize these hidden beliefs, but understanding one's own 'standpoint' is crucial because it influences research choices and methods. Personal feelings like joy or anger can even unconsciously shape research designs. Third, it's important not to overlook the commonplace in research. While precise definitions of 'leisure' or 'family' might not be the most critical aspect, what people actually do together in their daily lives is central. Life isn't just about big events like theme park visits; it's made up of everyday activities like dinner conversations, family vacations, doing yard work, joking around, caring for each other, relaxing, dreaming, and all the small details of each day and hour. These real-life, everyday conditions are what truly matter to everyone.
