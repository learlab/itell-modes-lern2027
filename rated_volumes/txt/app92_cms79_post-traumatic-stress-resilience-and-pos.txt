Post-Traumatic Stress, Resilience and Post Traumatic Growth

This document explores Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), Resilience, and Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG), detailing their definitions, interrelationships, differences, and strategies for prevention and intervention.

# Understanding Post-Traumatic Stress, Resilience, and Growth

## Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder: Definition and Causes

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is understood as a psychological injury arising from traumatic experiences or events. A 2016 study projected that 10 percent of veterans would develop PTSD, with psychiatric consequences taking an average of 40 years to address. While many individuals initially recover from trauma-related symptoms without treatment, those diagnosed with PTSD often experience worsening symptoms if left untreated . A wide range of events can cause PTSD, including war, natural disasters, severe accidents, terrorist attacks, the sudden death of a loved one, and various forms of abuse or neglect. The medical model frames PTSD around 'life-threat' trauma, where a single event triggers a fight, flight, or freeze response, often treated effectively with exposure therapies. As described by Helpguide.org, a normal response to trauma becomes PTSD when one remains 'stuck' in psychological shock, with memories and emotions disconnected.

## PTSD in Military Contexts and Symptoms

Beyond single 'life-threat' events, military contexts present unique traumatic situations contributing to PTSD. These include cumulative 'wear-and-tear' from prolonged stress and multiple deployments, and moral injury resulting from participating in or witnessing atrocities. These complex forms often require specialized treatment approaches not always available. PTSD is characterized by four symptom clusters: Re-experiencing : Nightmares or flashbacks of the traumatic event. Avoidance and Numbing : Actively avoiding thoughts, feelings, or places associated with the trauma. Hyperarousal and Reactivity : Sleep disturbances, being easily startled, or exaggerated responses. Negative Thoughts and Mood Changes : Persistent negative self-perceptions, distorted feelings of guilt, or general negative mood. In military and other settings, PTSD can develop gradually. Individuals with pre-clinical symptoms or problems after trauma, though not fully diagnosed with PTSD, may still experience significant impairment. Addressing these early symptoms is crucial, as the risk of developing full-blown PTSD increases if they remain unaddressed.

## Resilience: Definitions and Characteristics

Resilience is commonly defined as a personal attribute enabling an individual to remain relatively stable psychologically and physically despite traumatic events. It can also be seen as an 'outcome pattern' of healthy functioning post-trauma. Generally, resilient individuals are those who navigate traumatic events without developing severe PTSD symptoms, displaying a notable hardiness. Bensimon (2012) proposed three frameworks for understanding resilience: Recovery or Adaptation : Returning to a pre-trauma state, often termed 'bouncing back' or 'bouncing forward.' Individuals may experience minor PTSD symptoms, but these do not exceed normal levels. Resistance : Actively preventing destructive behavioral patterns from developing due to trauma, maintaining psychological balance. This highlights that certain personality types may better resist trauma, without implying flaws in those who develop PTSD. Reconfiguration : Involves cognitive processing to adapt and change, which significantly overlaps with Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG) . Resilience is a complex, multi-faceted construct, not a static trait; an individual might be resilient in one area, like work, but not another, like family life. It stems from many interacting factors and can apply beyond individuals to families, organizations, and even societies.

## Post-Traumatic Growth: Concept and Principles

Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG) , developed by R. G. Tedeschi (2011) and colleagues, originates from positive psychology and is explored as both a prevention and intervention strategy for PTSD. PTG signifies an individual's growth beyond their pre-trauma state following exposure to trauma. Key to PTG is challenging one's core beliefs after a traumatic event. This disruption can create opportunities for both stress and growth. Research suggests that a greater challenge to core beliefs increases the likelihood of PTG. Importantly, trauma itself does not cause PTG; rather, it's the cognitive work involved in processing the trauma's psychological impact that facilitates growth. Another crucial concept is rumination , which has two styles: Intrusive rumination : Characterized by distressing thoughts, often linked to increased PTSD likelihood. Deliberate rumination : A purposeful process of understanding and problem-solving, strongly associated with PTG. The style of rumination is vital for PTSD diagnosis, treatment, and fostering PTG. A study by Morgan et al. (2017) found both intrusive and deliberate rumination in veterans with PTSD and PTG. However, a stronger link to PTG emerged when veterans engaged in deliberate, cognitive processing of trauma. It's important to note that PTSD and PTG can coexist, and growth can occur even amidst significant distress.

## Domains of Post-Traumatic Growth

Tedeschi (2011) identified five domains where Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG) is typically observed: New Opportunities : Perceiving new possibilities emerging from the traumatic experience. Relationship Changes : Developing closer bonds with some individuals and potentially a greater empathy for those who suffer. Personal Strength : Gaining a profound belief in one's own capabilities, often expressed as 'If I could survive this, I can face anything.' Appreciation for Life : Experiencing a heightened sense of gratitude and value for life. Spiritual/Existential Change : Undergoing a significant transformation in one's belief system, often leading to greater spirituality. It's crucial to understand that PTG does not imply trauma is positive, but rather acknowledges it as an inevitable aspect of life, especially in contexts like military service in a war zone. Furthermore, PTG is not a universal outcome; it is unrealistic to expect every individual who experiences trauma or PTSD to achieve post-traumatic growth.

## Approaches for Preventing and Treating PTSD

Extensive resources are dedicated by the military, researchers, and clinicians to prevent and treat PTSD, with a dual focus on enhancing resilience and Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG) . Efforts are concentrated on two main fronts: psychologically-based programs and pharmacological solutions. Research by Horn et al. (2016) highlights several psychosocial factors linked to adult resilience that can also promote PTG and aid in PTSD prevention and treatment. These include: Cognitive reappraisal and emotion regulation skills. The ability to confront fears without being overwhelmed. Problem-solving and task orientation. Physical activity and humor. Accessing social capital and support networks. Many of these factors are teachable and learnable, making them viable targets for interventions aimed at Service members and veterans. The U.S. Military has already integrated such principles into resilience-building programs for active duty personnel, exemplified by the Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness program, which leverages Positive Psychology to develop personal strengths and adaptive attitudes.

## Psychologically-Based Programs for PTSD

Intervention programs can also enhance psychosocial factors for veterans with PTSD, even though they may not be initially labeled resilient. Resilience is viewed as a continuum, suggesting that these factors can be maximized to foster greater resilience and Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG) . Key interventions often focus on cognitive reappraisal, social support, physical activity, positive emotions, and finding meaning post-trauma. A notable resilience skills intervention is the Goal-Directed Resilience in Training (GRIT) program (Kent et al., 2015). GRIT helps individuals manage PTSD symptoms while pursuing goal-directed behaviors aligned with their values. A randomized-controlled trial showed that GRIT improved PTSD symptoms and overall well-being. Other promising treatment programs for PTSD prevention and treatment include: Life skills education : Focuses on problem-solving, decision-making, and emotion regulation. Meaning-making activities : Helps integrate trauma into one's life philosophy. Mindfulness : Teaches present-moment awareness of sensations and thoughts. Yoga : Integrates physical activity with body mindfulness, often paired with meditation. These strategies can promote PTG independently or alongside PTSD treatment. However, adequate cognitive functioning is a prerequisite; individuals with traumatic brain injury or substance use issues may not benefit until those conditions are resolved.

## Pharmacological Solutions and Conclusion

Alongside psychological interventions, pharmacological solutions are an emerging area of research for PTSD treatment. Horn et al. (2016) reviewed studies on PTSD's impact on hormonal, genetic, and immune functioning, highlighting these as potential avenues for treatment, either standalone or combined with psychosocial therapies. In conclusion, Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) remains a significant concern for military personnel and veterans. Both resilience and Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG) are crucial for prevention and recovery. The efficacy of both psychological and pharmacological interventions in treating PTSD and fostering growth is increasingly evident. A comprehensive intervention model is essential to address the intertwined nature of PTSD and PTG. This model should integrate: A deep understanding of trauma. Enhanced emotion regulation skills. The process of narrating one's story. Connecting this narrative to the five domains of PTG. Developing robust life principles to manage future challenges (Tedeschi, 2011).
